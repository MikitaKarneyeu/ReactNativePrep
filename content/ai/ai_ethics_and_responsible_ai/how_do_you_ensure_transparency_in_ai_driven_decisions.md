Transparency in AI means making the system's behavior, decisions, and limitations understandable to users, regulators, and other stakeholders. This encompasses multiple levels: algorithmic transparency (how the model works), decision transparency (why a specific prediction was made), and system transparency (what data is used, what the system can and cannot do).

For users, provide clear explanations of AI-driven decisions. When a loan application is denied, explain the key factors that influenced the decision. When content is recommended, indicate why it was suggested. Use natural language explanations rather than technical jargon. Implement "explain this decision" features that allow users to understand specific outcomes. Be transparent about uncertainty—"The model is 75% confident in this diagnosis" is more honest than presenting uncertain predictions as facts.

Model interpretability techniques provide explanations for predictions. SHAP (SHapley Additive exPlanations) values show how much each feature contributed to a prediction. LIME (Local Interpretable Model-agnostic Explanations) creates simple local approximations of complex models. Attention visualization shows which parts of the input the model focused on. Feature importance rankings identify the most influential factors. These techniques help developers debug models and help users understand decisions.

System-level transparency includes documenting model capabilities, limitations, and intended use cases. Model cards (standardized documentation) describe model performance, training data, fairness evaluations, and known limitations. Data datasheets document dataset characteristics, collection methods, and potential biases. Terms of service should clearly state when AI is used and how it affects users. Regulatory compliance (EU AI Act, local regulations) may require specific transparency disclosures.

Implementation practices include: labeling AI-generated content (disclosing when chatbots, recommendations, or content are AI-generated), providing confidence scores with predictions, enabling users to contest or appeal AI decisions, and maintaining audit logs of model behavior. Transparency should be proportional to the stakes—higher-risk applications (medical, legal, financial) require more detailed explanations and human oversight than lower-risk applications (content recommendations).
