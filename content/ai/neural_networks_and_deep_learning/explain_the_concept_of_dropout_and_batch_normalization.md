Dropout is a regularization technique that randomly deactivates a fraction of neurons during each training iteration. At each forward pass, each neuron has a probability p (typically 0.1-0.5) of being "dropped out"—its output is set to zero. This forces the network to learn redundant representations, as it cannot rely on any single neuron being present. At inference time, all neurons are active but their outputs are scaled by (1-p) to compensate for the increased number of active neurons.

Dropout prevents co-adaptation—neurons learning to correct for specific other neurons rather than learning generally useful features. It's equivalent to training an exponential number of thinned networks and averaging their predictions. This significantly reduces overfitting, especially in large networks with many parameters. Dropout is typically applied between fully connected layers and is less commonly used in convolutional layers (where spatial dropout or dropblock is preferred).

Batch normalization normalizes the inputs to each layer across the mini-batch, then applies a learnable scale and shift. For each mini-batch, it computes mean and variance, normalizes to zero mean and unit variance, then applies: `y = γ * (x - μ) / √(σ² + ε) + β`, where γ and β are learned parameters. This stabilizes training by reducing internal covariate shift—the phenomenon where the distribution of layer inputs changes as previous layers' parameters update.

Batch normalization provides several benefits: it allows higher learning rates, reduces sensitivity to weight initialization, acts as a regularizer (reducing the need for dropout), and helps with the vanishing gradient problem. It's applied before or after the activation function (the order is debated, though applying before activation is common in modern architectures). In practice, batch normalization and dropout are complementary—batch norm stabilizes training and provides some regularization, while dropout adds explicit regularization for overparameterized models. Modern architectures often use batch norm with reduced or no dropout, relying on data augmentation and weight decay for additional regularization.
