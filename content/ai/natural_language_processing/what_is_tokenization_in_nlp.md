Tokenization is the process of breaking text into smaller units called tokens—these can be words, subwords, or characters. It's the first step in any NLP pipeline, converting raw text into a format that models can process. The choice of tokenization strategy significantly impacts model performance, vocabulary size, and the ability to handle rare or unknown words.

Word-level tokenization splits text on whitespace and punctuation. "I don't like apples" becomes ["I", "don't", "'", "t", "like", "apples"]. This is simple but creates large vocabularies and struggles with out-of-vocabulary (OOV) words. Different languages have different word boundaries—Chinese and Japanese don't use spaces, requiring specialized tokenizers like Jieba or MeCab.

Subword tokenization is the modern standard, used by BERT, GPT, and most Transformer models. Byte-Pair Encoding (BPE) starts with individual characters and iteratively merges the most frequent pairs into new tokens. WordPiece (used in BERT) is similar but uses a likelihood-based merging criterion. Unigram (used in T5) starts with a large vocabulary and iteratively removes tokens. These approaches balance vocabulary size with token granularity—"unhappiness" might be split into ["un", "happi", "ness"], allowing the model to handle rare words and morphological variations.

Character-level tokenization treats each character as a token, resulting in very small vocabularies but very long sequences. Byte-level tokenization operates on raw bytes, handling any text including emojis and special characters without an unknown token. Modern LLMs like GPT use byte-level BPE with vocabularies of 50,000-100,000 tokens. The trade-off is between vocabulary size (memory), sequence length (computation), and token granularity (semantics). In practice, subword tokenization provides the best balance for most applications.
