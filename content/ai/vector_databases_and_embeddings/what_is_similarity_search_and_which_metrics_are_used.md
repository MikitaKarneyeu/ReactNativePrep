Similarity search finds the vectors in a database that are most similar to a query vector. Given a query embedding, the system returns the top-k nearest neighbors ranked by a distance or similarity metric. This is the fundamental operation powering semantic search, recommendation systems, and RAG retrieval.

Cosine similarity measures the cosine of the angle between two vectors, ranging from -1 (opposite) to 1 (identical). It's the most common metric for text embeddings because it captures directional similarity regardless of magnitude. Two documents about the same topic might have different lengths but point in the same direction in embedding space, resulting in high cosine similarity. It's computed as: `cosine_sim(A, B) = (A · B) / (||A|| * ||B||)`.

Euclidean distance (L2 distance) measures the straight-line distance between two points in vector space. Smaller values indicate higher similarity. It's sensitive to magnitude—a longer document might have a larger embedding vector even if the content is similar. Euclidean distance works well when embeddings are normalized or when magnitude carries meaningful information.

Dot product (inner product) computes `A · B = Σ(a_i * b_i)`. For normalized vectors (unit length), dot product equals cosine similarity. It's the fastest to compute and is preferred when vectors are already normalized. Some embedding models are designed to work with dot product as the primary metric.

Choosing the right metric depends on your embedding model and use case. Models trained with cosine similarity loss produce embeddings best compared with cosine similarity. Models trained with contrastive loss may work better with dot product. In practice, most text embedding models (OpenAI, Sentence-Transformers) produce normalized or near-normalized vectors, making cosine similarity and dot product nearly equivalent. Vector databases support all three metrics—choose the one that matches your embedding model's training objective. For most text applications, cosine similarity is the safe default.
