Chunking is the process of splitting documents into smaller passages for embedding and retrieval. The chunking strategy significantly impacts RAG quality—chunks that are too small lose context, while chunks that are too large contain irrelevant information that dilutes the embedding's focus.

Optimal chunk size depends on your content and use case. For most applications, 200-500 tokens (roughly 300-750 words) works well. Smaller chunks (100-200 tokens) provide more precise retrieval but may lack context. Larger chunks (500-1000 tokens) provide more context but may be less precisely matched. Experiment with different sizes and evaluate retrieval quality on representative queries.

Chunk at natural boundaries. Split documents at paragraph, section, or heading boundaries rather than arbitrary character counts. This keeps related information together. For structured documents (markdown, HTML), use the document structure—each section or subsection becomes a chunk. For unstructured text, use paragraph breaks or semantic similarity between sentences to find natural split points.

Include overlapping text between adjacent chunks. When you split at a boundary, include the last 50-100 tokens of the previous chunk at the beginning of the next chunk. This prevents information that spans a boundary from being lost—the end of one chunk and the beginning of the next both contain the bridging information.

Preserve metadata with each chunk. Include the source document title, section heading, page number, and URL. This enables citations ("According to section 3.2 of the API documentation...") and allows users to verify information. Include hierarchical context—add the parent section or chapter title to each chunk so the embedding captures the topic context.

Advanced chunking strategies include semantic chunking (splitting based on topic changes rather than fixed sizes), recursive chunking (splitting large chunks into smaller ones until reaching the target size), and parent-child chunking (retrieving small precise chunks but returning the larger parent chunk for context). For tables and code, preserve the complete structure—don't split a table across chunks. For FAQs and Q&A documents, keep question-answer pairs together. Evaluate chunking quality by testing retrieval on real user queries and checking if the retrieved chunks contain the correct answers.
