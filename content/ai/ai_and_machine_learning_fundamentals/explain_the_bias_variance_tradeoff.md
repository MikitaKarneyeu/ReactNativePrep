The bias-variance tradeoff is a fundamental concept describing the tension between two sources of error in machine learning models. Bias measures how far off the model's predictions are from the correct values on average—high bias means the model makes strong assumptions and consistently misses relevant patterns (underfitting). Variance measures how much the model's predictions change across different training sets—high variance means the model is sensitive to training data fluctuations (overfitting).

Total model error can be decomposed as: Error = Bias² + Variance + Irreducible Noise. Irreducible noise is inherent in the data and cannot be reduced. The goal is to find the sweet spot where the sum of bias and variance is minimized. Simple models (linear regression) have high bias but low variance. Complex models (deep neural networks, high-degree polynomials) have low bias but high variance.

The tradeoff manifests in practice: as you increase model complexity, training error decreases (lower bias) but test error follows a U-shaped curve—it decreases initially as bias drops, then increases as variance dominates. The optimal complexity minimizes test error. This is why model selection and hyperparameter tuning are critical—they find the right complexity level for the data.

Regularization techniques explicitly manage this tradeoff. L2 regularization shrinks weights toward zero, increasing bias slightly but reducing variance significantly. Ensemble methods address it differently: bagging (Random Forest) reduces variance by averaging multiple high-variance models, while boosting reduces bias by sequentially correcting errors. In deep learning, the bias-variance tradeoff is managed through architecture design, dropout, batch normalization, and data augmentation. Modern large language models have very high capacity (low bias) but require massive datasets and regularization to control variance.
