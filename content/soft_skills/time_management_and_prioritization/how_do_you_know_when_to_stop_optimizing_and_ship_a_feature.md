Knowing when to stop optimizing is a judgment call that balances quality with pragmatism. My guiding principle is: ship when the feature meets the defined acceptance criteria and provides user value, then iterate based on real feedback.

I start by defining "done" before I begin coding. What are the acceptance criteria? What performance benchmarks are acceptable? What edge cases must be handled versus what can be addressed later? If the requirement is "users can search products," the done state is a functional, reasonably fast search—not a search with fuzzy matching, typo correction, autocomplete, and personalization. Those are improvements for future iterations.

I use the concept of diminishing returns. Early optimization work yields high value—fixing a query that takes 10 seconds to take 200ms is critical. But optimizing from 200ms to 150ms might not be worth the extra day of development. I ask: "Will the user notice this improvement? Will it meaningfully impact the business metric we're targeting?" If the answer is no, I ship and move on.

I also consider the cost of not shipping. Every day a feature sits in development is a day it's not providing value to users or generating feedback. Real-world usage often reveals different priorities than development-time optimization. Users might not care about the performance improvement I'm spending a week on, but they might desperately need a feature I haven't started because I was polishing.

There are legitimate reasons to optimize further before shipping: security vulnerabilities, data integrity issues, or performance that's genuinely unacceptable (a page that takes 10 seconds to load). But "I could make this code more elegant" or "this could be 10% faster" are usually reasons to ship now and iterate later. I follow the principle: make it work, make it right, make it fast—in that order. Once it works and is correct, shipping it and getting real-world feedback is almost always more valuable than continuing to polish in isolation.
