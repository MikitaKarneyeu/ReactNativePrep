Effective sprint planning starts well before the planning meeting. My approach involves preparation, collaborative estimation, and realistic commitment.

Before the planning meeting, I review the prioritized backlog with the PM to make sure the top items are well-defined. I look for missing acceptance criteria, ambiguous requirements, or technical unknowns that need to be resolved before we can estimate accurately. If a story needs a technical spike—research or prototyping to understand feasibility—I flag it and suggest scheduling the spike in the current sprint so the actual work can be planned in the next.

During planning, I advocate for task breakdown as a team activity. The developer who will do the work should break it down, but doing it collaboratively catches gaps. I use a structure: what's the user-facing outcome? What are the technical steps to get there? What are the edge cases? What testing is needed? A story like "Add email notifications for order confirmation" might break down into: design the email template, implement the email service, integrate with the order completion event, handle failures and retries, write unit and integration tests, and update the documentation.

For estimation, I prefer story points over hours because points account for complexity and uncertainty, not just effort. A task might be 2 hours of coding but 5 story points because it involves unfamiliar territory. I use planning poker to encourage discussion—when estimates diverge significantly, it usually means people have different understandings of the scope, which is valuable to surface.

For sprint commitment, I use historical velocity as a guide. If the team has consistently completed 30-35 story points per sprint for the last three sprints, I don't commit to 50. I also leave buffer—about 15-20%—for unexpected work (production issues, urgent requests, dependency blockers). A sprint that's perfectly packed leaves no room for reality. I'd rather commit to less and deliver reliably than overcommit and consistently miss targets, which erodes trust with stakeholders.
