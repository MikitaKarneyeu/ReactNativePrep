When everything feels urgent, I step back and apply a framework to create objective ordering. The feeling that "everything is urgent" usually means prioritization hasn't been explicitly done—it's a signal to pause and categorize, not to work faster.

I use a modified Eisenhower matrix tailored for engineering. I evaluate each item on two dimensions: business impact (what happens if this isn't done?) and time sensitivity (is there a hard deadline?). This creates four categories: do now (high impact, time-sensitive), schedule (high impact, not time-sensitive), delegate or quick-win (low impact, time-sensitive), and deprioritize (low impact, not time-sensitive). Critically, I involve the PM or stakeholders in this classification—what feels urgent to a developer (refactoring a messy module) might not be urgent from a business perspective, and vice versa.

I also ask the "what breaks?" question for each item. A production bug affecting users is genuinely urgent. A feature request from a big client with a contract deadline is urgent. A nice-to-have improvement that someone asked for loudly in Slack is probably not urgent, even if it feels like it. I quantify impact where possible: how many users are affected? What's the revenue impact? What's the cost of delay?

When I truly have multiple items with equal urgency and impact, I look for sequencing optimizations. Can I combine two tasks into one effort? Is there a dependency where completing one unblocks others? I also consider context-switching cost—switching between related tasks is cheaper than jumping between completely different codebases.

Finally, I communicate the prioritization and the reasoning to stakeholders. "Here are the five things on my plate. Based on impact and deadlines, here's the order I plan to tackle them. Does this align with your expectations?" This invites course correction early and prevents surprises. Often, stakeholders realize they don't actually need everything right now—they just hadn't been forced to rank their requests.
