Building relationships with non-technical team members starts with genuine curiosity about their work and respect for their expertise. I've found that the best technical people are those who understand the business, not just the code.

I invest time in understanding what non-technical colleagues do and what challenges they face. I ask designers about their process, PMs about how they prioritize, and salespeople about what customers are saying. This isn't small talk—it's context that makes me a better developer. When I understand that a designer's biggest frustration is developers changing their layouts without asking, I'm more likely to communicate proactively when I need to deviate from a design.

I communicate in their language, not mine. When talking to a PM, I frame things in terms of user impact and timelines, not in terms of technical implementation. When talking to a designer, I frame things in terms of visual fidelity and user experience, not CSS frameworks. I avoid jargon unless I'm sure the audience understands it, and I'm always willing to explain technical concepts in plain language.

I follow through on commitments. If I tell a PM I'll have something by Thursday, I deliver by Thursday—or communicate early if I can't. Reliability builds trust faster than brilliance. Non-technical team members often have bad experiences with developers who overpromise and underdeliver, so being consistently reliable is a powerful relationship builder.

I also show genuine appreciation for their contributions. A designer who creates a beautiful, intuitive UX makes my job easier. A PM who writes clear requirements saves me hours of confusion. A QA tester who catches a bug before it reaches users saves me from a production incident. I acknowledge these contributions publicly and thank people privately. This creates a positive dynamic where we see each other as partners rather than obstacles.

Finally, I include non-technical team members in technical discussions when appropriate. Inviting a PM to a technical design review (and explaining things in accessible language) helps them understand the complexity of what we're building and makes them more effective at prioritizing. Inviting a designer to a frontend architecture discussion helps them understand the constraints and opportunities. These inclusions build shared understanding and stronger working relationships.
