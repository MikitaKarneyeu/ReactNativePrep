Early in a project, I chose to use a microservices architecture for what was essentially a CRUD application with moderate traffic. My reasoning at the time was that the product was expected to grow significantly, and I wanted the architecture to be ready for scale. I was also influenced by industry trends—microservices were the dominant pattern in the engineering content I was consuming.

Within three months, the decision was clearly wrong. The overhead of managing multiple services—separate deployments, inter-service communication, distributed debugging, data consistency—consumed more engineering time than the actual feature development. The team was small (four developers), and the operational complexity of microservices was overwhelming them. We were spending 40% of our time on infrastructure and operations instead of building features.

I owned the mistake and proposed a correction. I presented the data to the team and my manager: our velocity had dropped significantly since the microservices migration, and the theoretical scalability benefits weren't needed—our traffic was well within what a modular monolith could handle. I proposed consolidating the services into a well-structured monolith with clear module boundaries, so we could split into services later if we actually needed to.

The team agreed, and we spent three weeks consolidating. The result was dramatic: deployment complexity dropped (one service instead of six), debugging became straightforward (one log stream, one process), and our feature velocity doubled. The monolith was designed with clear module boundaries, so if we ever needed to extract a service, we could do it surgically.

The lessons I took from this decision: don't architect for scale you don't have—premature optimization applies to architecture too. Evaluate trends critically—just because a pattern works for Netflix doesn't mean it works for a four-person team. And when you realize a decision was wrong, act on it quickly—the longer you wait, the more expensive the correction becomes. I now evaluate architectural decisions against current needs, not hypothetical future needs, and I explicitly consider team size and operational capacity as constraints.
