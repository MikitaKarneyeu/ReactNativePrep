Adopting a new tool or library is a decision with long-term consequences, so I evaluate it systematically rather than being swayed by hype or novelty. My evaluation framework considers problem fit, maturity, cost, and team impact.

First, I clarify the problem I'm trying to solve. A new tool should address a specific, real pain point—not just be newer or more popular than what I'm using. I ask: what's wrong with our current approach? What specific capability does the new tool provide that we lack? If I can't articulate a concrete problem, I don't adopt a new tool.

Next, I evaluate maturity and sustainability. I check: how active is the project? Are releases regular? How many contributors are there? Is there a company or foundation backing it? What's the community size? I look at GitHub activity, npm download trends, and the quality of documentation. I also check for known security vulnerabilities and how quickly they're patched. A library that's popular but abandoned is worse than a less popular one that's actively maintained.

I assess the migration and adoption cost. How different is the new tool from what we currently use? How much code needs to change? How long will it take the team to learn it? What's the risk if we need to switch away later? I prefer tools that can be adopted incrementally—introduced for a small feature first, evaluated in production, and then expanded if successful. Tools that require a big-bang migration are risky unless the benefits are overwhelming.

Finally, I build a proof of concept. I create a small, isolated implementation that tests the tool against our specific use case—not the toy examples from the documentation. I measure performance, developer experience, and integration with our existing stack. I share the results with the team and get their input. Adopting a tool is a team decision, not an individual one, because the whole team will live with the consequences. I document the evaluation and decision in an ADR so the reasoning is preserved.
