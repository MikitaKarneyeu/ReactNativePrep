Early in my career, I was responsible for implementing a caching layer for an API that served product data for an e-commerce site. I designed a simple in-memory cache with a 5-minute TTL and deployed it to production. What I didn't account for was cache stampede—when the cache expired during a traffic spike, hundreds of concurrent requests hit the database simultaneously, causing a 30-second outage.

The immediate mistake was technical: I hadn't implemented any protection against cache stampede (locking, stale-while-revalidate, or probabilistic early expiration). But the deeper mistake was process: I hadn't load-tested the caching layer under realistic conditions, I hadn't considered what would happen during cache misses at scale, and I hadn't consulted with more experienced developers who might have flagged the risk.

I owned the mistake immediately. I told my manager what happened, explained the root cause, and proposed a fix. I implemented a stale-while-revalidate pattern: when the cache expires, serve the stale data while refreshing the cache in the background. This eliminated the stampede entirely. I also added load testing to our CI pipeline so similar issues would be caught before reaching production.

The lessons from this failure have stayed with me. First, always consider failure modes, not just happy paths. When designing a system, ask: what happens when this component fails? What happens under 10x normal load? What happens when an external dependency is slow or unavailable? Second, consult with experienced colleagues when working on critical infrastructure—their experience with failure modes you haven't encountered is invaluable. Third, invest in testing that simulates real-world conditions, not just unit tests that verify correct behavior under ideal conditions.

I now approach every system design with a "what could go wrong?" mindset. I write failure scenarios alongside success scenarios. I test for edge cases and load conditions. And I never assume that because something works in development, it will work in production. This failure made me a significantly better engineer, and I'm grateful it happened early in my career when the stakes were relatively low.
