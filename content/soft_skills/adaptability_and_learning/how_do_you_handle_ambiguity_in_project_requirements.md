Ambiguity in requirements is the norm, not the exception. My approach is to reduce ambiguity iteratively—gather what information is available, make reasonable assumptions, build incrementally, and refine as clarity emerges.

First, I identify what's known and what's unknown. I break the requirement into specific questions: Who is the user? What are they trying to do? What does success look like? What are the edge cases? I document these questions and get answers from the PM, designer, or stakeholders. Some questions can be answered immediately; others need research or user feedback. I prioritize getting clarity on questions that affect architecture or design decisions, because those are expensive to change later.

For questions that can't be answered immediately, I make explicit assumptions. I document them: "I'm assuming we'll support up to 10,000 concurrent users. If the actual number is significantly different, we may need to adjust the architecture." Making assumptions explicit serves two purposes: it lets me move forward without waiting for perfect information, and it creates a checklist to validate as more information becomes available.

I build in a way that's resilient to change. If I'm unsure about a UI requirement, I build the backend with a flexible API that can support multiple frontend approaches. If I'm unsure about scale requirements, I choose architectures that can be scaled up later without rewriting (horizontal scaling over vertical, for example). I favor decisions that are easy to reverse—choosing a library over building custom, using interfaces over concrete implementations, and keeping components loosely coupled.

I also use iterative delivery to reduce ambiguity. Instead of trying to build the perfect solution based on ambiguous requirements, I build a minimal version, get feedback, and iterate. This is especially effective when the ambiguity is about user behavior—how users will actually use a feature is often unclear until they can try it. By delivering incrementally, I turn ambiguity into a series of smaller, more manageable decisions rather than one big guess.
