Balancing technical leadership with individual contribution is one of the hardest transitions for senior engineers. I've found that the key is being intentional about where I spend my time and being willing to let go of tasks that others can do.

I allocate my time explicitly. Roughly 60% goes to hands-on development—the complex features, critical bug fixes, and architectural work where my experience adds the most value. The remaining 40% goes to leadership activities: code reviews, mentoring, technical planning, writing documentation, and facilitating discussions. I track this loosely and adjust when the balance drifts too far in either direction.

For individual contribution, I'm selective about what I take on. I focus on work that has high technical complexity or high leverage—setting up the architecture for a new service, tackling the hardest performance issue, or building the foundational library that other developers will use. I avoid taking on tasks that are routine or that would be good growth opportunities for junior developers. If I'm the only one who can do a task, that's a red flag—I should be teaching others to do it.

For leadership, I focus on activities that multiply the team's output rather than adding to my own. A code review that catches a bug and teaches the author something is more valuable than me writing the code myself. An hour spent mentoring a junior developer who then independently handles features they couldn't before is a better investment than me doing that work. I'm deliberate about saying "no" to individual work when my leadership time is needed.

I also set boundaries. I don't try to review every PR—I focus on the ones where my feedback will have the most impact (junior developers, complex changes, areas where I have domain expertise). I don't attend every meeting—I prioritize ones where my input is critical and send written input for the rest. The goal is to be a force multiplier: if my leadership activities help the team deliver 20% more, that's worth more than me delivering 10% more individually.
