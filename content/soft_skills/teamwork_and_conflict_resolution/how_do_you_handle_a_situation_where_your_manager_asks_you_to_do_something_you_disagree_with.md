When my manager asks me to do something I disagree with, my first step is to understand the full context. Managers often have information I don't—business pressures, organizational politics, strategic priorities, or constraints from other teams. What seems like a bad decision from my technical perspective might be the right call from a broader business perspective.

I ask clarifying questions to understand the "why": "Can you help me understand the reasoning behind this? I want to make sure I'm approaching it the right way." This often reveals context that changes my perspective. If the reasoning still doesn't make sense to me, I respectfully share my concerns with specific technical or business reasoning. "I'm concerned that this approach will introduce a security vulnerability because of X. Have we considered Y alternative?"

If my manager listens but still wants to proceed, I follow the "disagree and commit" principle. I voice my concern clearly and document it if appropriate, then execute the request to the best of my ability. Sabotaging a decision you disagree with—through passive resistance, minimal effort, or "malicious compliance"—is unprofessional and damages trust.

However, there are lines. If the request involves something unethical (cutting security corners that endanger users, falsifying metrics, or violating regulations), I would escalate through appropriate channels. Disagreeing on technical approach is normal; being asked to do something unethical is not.

After executing the decision, I monitor the outcomes. If my concerns prove valid, I share the data objectively—not with "I told you so" but with "Here's what we observed—should we consider adjusting?" This builds credibility for future decisions. If my concerns prove unfounded, I learn from it and update my mental model. Over time, this cycle of respectful disagreement followed by committed execution builds a relationship where my manager actively seeks my input because they know I'll be honest and constructive.
