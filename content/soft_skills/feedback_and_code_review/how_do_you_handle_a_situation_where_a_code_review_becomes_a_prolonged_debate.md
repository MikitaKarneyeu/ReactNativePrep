Prolonged debates in code reviews are a sign that the discussion has moved beyond the code into preferences, principles, or communication breakdowns. My approach is to recognize when a debate is no longer productive and take steps to resolve it.

First, I assess whether the debate is about a subjective preference or an objective concern. "I prefer arrow functions" versus "this approach will cause a race condition" are fundamentally different types of disagreement. Subjective preferences should be resolved by deferring to the team's style guide or the author's preference—the reviewer can suggest but shouldn't block. Objective concerns need to be addressed with evidence.

If the debate is about a genuine technical disagreement, I suggest moving it out of the PR comments. A 20-comment thread on a PR is painful for everyone involved and creates an adversarial dynamic. I suggest a quick call or in-person discussion: "We seem to have different perspectives on this. Can we hop on a 10-minute call to discuss? I think we'll resolve this faster face-to-face." Real-time conversation is almost always more effective than asynchronous text for resolving disagreements.

During the discussion, I focus on the criteria for making the decision. Instead of debating endlessly about which approach is "better," I ask: "What are the criteria that matter most for this specific case? Performance? Readability? Future extensibility? Alignment with existing patterns?" Once we agree on the criteria, evaluating the options becomes more objective.

If we still can't agree after a direct conversation, I suggest involving a third party—a tech lead, architect, or another experienced developer. Having a neutral perspective often breaks the deadlock. I also suggest a time-box: "Let's try your approach for this PR, and if we hit the issues I'm concerned about, we'll revisit." The important thing is to resolve the debate and move forward. Perfectionism in code review blocks the team and creates resentment. I'd rather ship slightly imperfect code than let a PR sit in review for a week over a minor architectural preference.
