When I disagree with a product requirement, my first step is to make sure I fully understand the intent behind it. Requirements often represent a solution the PM has chosen, but the underlying goal may be something I can support. I ask clarifying questions: "What user problem are we solving?" and "What metrics are we trying to move?" This shifts the conversation from "I don't want to build this" to "Let's make sure we're solving the right problem."

If after understanding the intent I still have concerns, I present my perspective with data and alternatives rather than just opinions. For instance, a PM once requested an auto-save feature that would persist form data on every keystroke. I was concerned about database load and potential data integrity issues. Instead of saying "this won't work," I came with numbers: "With 50,000 concurrent users, this would generate 50,000 writes per second, which exceeds our database capacity. Here are two alternatives: debounce saves to every 5 seconds, or save on field blur. Both give users a near-identical experience with 95% fewer writes."

I also recognize that PMs have context I don't have—business strategy, competitive pressure, customer commitments. Sometimes a requirement that seems suboptimal from a technical lens is the right call from a business lens. In those cases, I express my concern once, document it, and then fully commit to the implementation. I don't sabotage a decision I disagree with by doing the minimum or by continuing to argue after a call has been made.

The key principle is to disagree and commit. Voice your concern constructively, provide alternatives with reasoning, but once a decision is made, execute it with your full effort. Over time, building credibility through good-faith engagement means your technical input carries more weight in future discussions.
