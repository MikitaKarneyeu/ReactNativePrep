Communicating technical debt to non-technical stakeholders requires framing it in terms they care about: risk, cost, speed, and user impact. I avoid using the term "technical debt" in isolation because it sounds abstract. Instead, I translate it into concrete business language.

My approach is to use a simple analogy: "Imagine you built a house quickly using cheap materials. It works fine now, but every time you want to renovate a room, it takes twice as long and costs twice as much because you have to work around the weak foundation. Technical debt is the same—it's shortcuts we took (often for good reasons, like meeting a deadline) that now slow down every new feature."

I back this up with specific, measurable examples. Instead of saying "our codebase has technical debt," I say: "Adding a new payment method currently takes 3 weeks instead of 1 week because our payment module is tightly coupled to the old provider. If we spend 2 weeks refactoring it, every future payment integration will be 3x faster." I quantify the cost of inaction versus the investment required. A simple table showing "current cost per feature" versus "cost after refactoring" makes the ROI tangible.

I also prioritize technical debt items by business impact. Not all debt is worth paying down. I categorize items as: blocking new features, causing production incidents, slowing developer velocity, or posing security/compliance risks. I present these categories to stakeholders and let them help prioritize. This makes them part of the decision rather than feeling like developers are asking for "time to clean up code." The goal is to make technical debt a shared business concern, not a developer-only complaint.
