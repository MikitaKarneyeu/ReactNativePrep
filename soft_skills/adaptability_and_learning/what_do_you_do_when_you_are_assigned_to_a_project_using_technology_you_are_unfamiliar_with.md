Being assigned to a project with unfamiliar technology is an opportunity, not a crisis. My approach is to be transparent about my learning curve, ramp up efficiently, and contribute value quickly even while I'm still learning.

First, I'm honest with my manager and team about my current level of experience. I don't pretend to know things I don't—this leads to bad decisions and wasted time. I say: "I haven't worked with this stack before, but here's my plan to get productive quickly, and here's where I can contribute immediately based on my existing skills." Most managers appreciate this honesty and will adjust expectations and provide support.

I ramp up using a layered approach. I start with the fundamentals—reading the official documentation, completing a basic tutorial, and understanding the core concepts. I don't try to learn everything at once; I focus on what's relevant to the project. Then I read the project's own codebase, which teaches me how the technology is used in practice within our specific context. I look at the project structure, the patterns used, and the testing approach. I also set up the development environment immediately and get the project running locally—hands-on experience accelerates learning faster than reading.

I find ways to contribute value quickly, even while I'm still learning the technology. I can write documentation, improve test coverage, fix bugs in areas that don't require deep domain knowledge, and review PRs for general code quality (readability, naming, structure). I also bring my existing expertise—if I'm experienced with performance optimization in one stack, those principles apply across technologies. I look for places where my transferable skills can add immediate value.

I also identify a mentor or buddy on the team who's experienced with the technology. I ask them targeted questions (not every question—I try to solve problems myself first) and pair-program with them on complex tasks. I've found that having a go-to person for context and guidance accelerates ramp-up from months to weeks. Within two to three weeks of focused effort, I'm typically productive enough to take on regular sprint tasks with minimal support.
