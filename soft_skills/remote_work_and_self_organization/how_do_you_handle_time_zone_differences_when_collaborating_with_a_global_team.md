Working across time zones requires a shift from synchronous to asynchronous-first communication, with synchronous meetings reserved for high-bandwidth discussions. My approach prioritizes documentation, overlap windows, and respect for everyone's working hours.

I default to asynchronous communication for most interactions. Instead of scheduling a meeting to discuss a design decision, I write it up in a shared document and invite feedback asynchronously. Instead of asking a question in Slack and expecting an immediate response, I provide full context and clearly state that a response within 24 hours is fine. This reduces the pressure on teammates in different time zones to be online at inconvenient hours.

For synchronous meetings, I find the overlap window—the hours when all time zones are reasonably available—and schedule critical meetings within that window. If the overlap is small (e.g., Pacific time and India time have minimal overlap), I rotate meeting times so the burden of attending at odd hours is shared fairly rather than always falling on the same team members. I also record meetings and share notes so that anyone who can't attend can catch up asynchronously.

I'm explicit about response time expectations. I use Slack status to indicate my working hours and current availability. I document decisions in writing rather than relying on verbal agreements in meetings that not everyone attends. I use tools like Notion or Confluence for documentation that's accessible to everyone regardless of when they work.

I also design my work to minimize blocking dependencies across time zones. If a teammate in another time zone needs my code review to continue their work, I prioritize reviewing it before I end my day so they have it when they start theirs. I try to structure my work so that my deliverables are available at the start of the next time zone's workday. This requires planning and discipline, but it prevents the frustrating cycle of "I asked a question, waited 12 hours for a response, then needed clarification, waited another 12 hours..." that plagues poorly managed global teams.
