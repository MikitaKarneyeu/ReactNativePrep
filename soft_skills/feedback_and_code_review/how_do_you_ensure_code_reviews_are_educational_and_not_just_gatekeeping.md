Code reviews become gatekeeping when they focus on asserting authority rather than improving code and growing developers. I intentionally design my review process to be educational by explaining reasoning, adapting to the author's level, and creating a two-way learning dynamic.

The most important practice is explaining the "why" behind every piece of feedback. Instead of "use a Map here," I say "A Map would be better here because you're doing frequent lookups by key, and Map has O(1) lookup while an array with `find` is O(n). With the number of items we're dealing with, this could affect performance." This teaches the author a principle they can apply to future code, not just a rule for this one line.

I adapt my feedback to the author's experience level. For junior developers, I provide more detailed explanations and link to learning resources. I might suggest they read a specific article on the pattern I'm recommending. For senior developers, I focus on architectural discussions and design trade-offs—they don't need me to explain what a Map is, but they might benefit from a discussion about when to optimize for readability versus performance.

I also learn from code reviews. When an author uses a pattern or approach I haven't seen, I ask them to explain it. "I noticed you used a discriminated union here—can you walk me through why this approach works well?" This creates a reciprocal dynamic where the reviewer isn't positioned as the expert who knows everything, but as a fellow developer who's engaging with the code. It also signals to junior developers that it's okay to have different approaches and that learning goes both ways.

I encourage authors to add context in their PR descriptions that explains their design decisions. This turns the review into a discussion about trade-offs rather than a one-directional critique. When I see a decision I disagree with, I ask about the reasoning first—maybe they considered and rejected the approach I was going to suggest, and their reason is valid. The goal is a review culture where both the author and the reviewer come away better than they went in.
