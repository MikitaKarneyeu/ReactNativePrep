Mentoring junior developers is one of the most impactful things a senior developer can do. My approach balances giving them enough support to succeed with giving them enough challenge to grow. The goal is to build their independence, not their dependence on me.

I start by understanding where they are. What do they know? Where are their gaps? What are their goals? A junior developer who's strong in JavaScript but struggles with system design needs different guidance than one who understands architecture but writes sloppy code. I tailor my mentoring to their specific needs rather than following a generic curriculum.

For day-to-day work, I use the "guide on the side" approach rather than "sage on the stage." When they ask me a question, I resist the urge to just give the answer. Instead, I ask leading questions: "What have you tried so far? What do you think might be happening? Where would you look to find out?" This teaches them the debugging and problem-solving process, not just the solution to one problem. I pair-program with them on complex tasks, narrating my thought process so they can see how an experienced developer approaches problems.

I give them tasks that stretch their abilities without overwhelming them. A task that's too easy doesn't help them grow; a task that's too hard leads to frustration. I look for the "zone of proximal development"—work that's slightly beyond their current comfort zone but achievable with some guidance. I also make sure they have ownership of real features, not just bug fixes or cleanup work. There's a common trap of giving juniors only low-value tasks, which limits their growth and disengages them.

I provide regular, specific feedback. Instead of "good job on that feature," I say "I liked how you handled the error states in that component—your approach with the try-catch block and user-friendly error messages was solid." For improvement areas, I'm specific and constructive: "Your variable naming could be more descriptive—instead of `d`, use `deliveryDate` so other developers understand the code without context." I also do periodic career conversations: what skills do they want to develop? What kind of work excites them? This helps me assign tasks that align with their growth goals.
