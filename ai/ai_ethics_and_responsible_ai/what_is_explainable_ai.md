Explainable AI (XAI) refers to methods and techniques that make AI model decisions understandable to humans. As AI systems are deployed in high-stakes domains—healthcare, criminal justice, finance, autonomous vehicles—understanding why a model made a specific decision becomes critical for trust, accountability, debugging, and regulatory compliance.

Global explainability means understanding the overall behavior of the model—what features it relies on, what patterns it has learned, and where it tends to fail. Techniques include feature importance rankings (which inputs most influence predictions), partial dependence plots (how a feature affects predictions on average), and decision tree visualization for simpler models. Global explanations help developers verify the model has learned appropriate patterns and identify potential biases.

Local explainability explains individual predictions. SHAP values provide a mathematically rigorous decomposition of each prediction into feature contributions. LIME creates a simple interpretable model (like linear regression) that approximates the complex model's behavior in the neighborhood of a specific instance. Attention mechanisms in Transformers show which input tokens the model focused on for each output. Counterfactual explanations describe the minimum change needed to flip a prediction—"Your loan would have been approved if your income were $5,000 higher."

XAI methods vary by model type. For linear models and decision trees, the model itself is interpretable—coefficients and splits directly show decision logic. For neural networks, post-hoc methods (SHAP, LIME, Grad-CAM for images) are needed because the model's internal representations aren't human-readable. There's a fundamental trade-off between model complexity and interpretability—more complex models often perform better but are harder to explain.

In practice, XAI is required by regulations (EU AI Act, GDPR's right to explanation) and is increasingly expected by users. Implement explanations that match your audience—technical details for developers, high-level summaries for end users, and audit trails for regulators. Be cautious about over-trusting explanations—SHAP and LIME provide approximations, not ground truth about the model's reasoning. Use explanations as debugging tools and communication aids, not as proof of correctness.
