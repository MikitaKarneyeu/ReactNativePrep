CNNs (Convolutional Neural Networks) are designed for spatial data processing, primarily images and video. They use convolutional filters that slide across the input, detecting local patterns like edges, textures, and shapes. Key properties include local connectivity (each neuron connects to a small region), weight sharing (the same filter is applied across the entire input), and translation invariance (a feature detected in one location can be detected anywhere). Pooling layers reduce spatial dimensions and provide some spatial invariance. CNNs process data in parallel, making them computationally efficient for fixed-size inputs.

RNNs (Recurrent Neural Networks) are designed for sequential data—text, speech, time series. They maintain a hidden state that captures information from previous time steps, processing one element at a time in sequence. The hidden state is updated at each step: `h_t = f(W_h * h_{t-1} + W_x * x_t)`. This makes RNNs naturally suited for variable-length sequences. However, vanilla RNNs suffer from vanishing gradients in long sequences. LSTM (Long Short-Term Memory) and GRU (Gated Recurrent Unit) address this with gating mechanisms that control information flow. RNNs process sequentially, limiting parallelization.

Transformers use self-attention mechanisms to process entire sequences in parallel. Instead of relying on sequential processing, attention computes relationships between all positions simultaneously: `Attention(Q, K, V) = softmax(QK^T / √d) * V`. This enables capturing long-range dependencies efficiently and allows massive parallelization during training. Positional encodings provide sequence order information since attention itself is permutation-invariant. Transformers have become the dominant architecture for NLP (BERT, GPT, T5) and are increasingly used for vision (ViT), audio, and multimodal tasks.

The key trade-offs: CNNs excel at spatial pattern recognition with computational efficiency. RNNs handle variable-length sequences with temporal dependencies but are slow to train. Transformers offer the best of both—parallel processing and long-range dependency capture—at the cost of quadratic memory complexity with sequence length (O(n²)). In practice, Transformers have largely replaced both CNNs and RNNs in state-of-the-art models, though CNNs remain popular for edge deployment and specific vision tasks where efficiency matters.
