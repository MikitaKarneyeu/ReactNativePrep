The vanishing gradient problem occurs when gradients become extremely small as they are backpropagated through many layers, effectively preventing early layers from learning. During backpropagation, gradients are multiplied by the derivative of the activation function at each layer. If these derivatives are consistently less than 1 (as with sigmoid or tanh), the product shrinks exponentially—after many layers, the gradient is negligibly small, and early layer weights barely update.

This problem is particularly severe with sigmoid and tanh activations. Sigmoid derivatives have a maximum of 0.25, so gradients shrink by at least 75% per layer. In a 10-layer network, gradients can be reduced by a factor of 0.25^10 ≈ 0.000001. The early layers, which should learn fundamental features, receive virtually no learning signal. This makes training deep networks with these activations extremely difficult or impossible.

Solutions include using ReLU activation, which has a gradient of 1 for positive inputs, allowing gradients to flow unchanged through the network. Residual connections (skip connections) in ResNet architecture allow gradients to bypass layers via identity mappings, providing a direct gradient path. Batch normalization stabilizes the distribution of layer inputs, reducing internal covariate shift and maintaining gradient magnitude. LSTM and GRU architectures use gating mechanisms specifically designed to maintain gradient flow through long sequences.

The exploding gradient problem is the opposite—gradients grow exponentially, causing unstable training. Gradient clipping (capping gradient magnitude) addresses this. In practice, modern architectures are designed to mitigate both problems: ReLU activations, residual connections, and normalization layers are standard components. The vanishing gradient problem was a major historical barrier to training deep networks and motivated much of the architectural innovation in deep learning, including the development of LSTM, ResNet, and Transformer architectures.
