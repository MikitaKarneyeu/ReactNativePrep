Word2Vec and GloVe are both word embedding techniques that produce dense vector representations of words, but they differ in their training approach. Word2Vec, developed by Mikolov et al. at Google, learns embeddings by predicting words from their local context. It uses a shallow neural network with two architectures: CBOW predicts a target word from surrounding context words, and Skip-gram predicts context words from a target word. Training is done on sliding windows across the corpus.

GloVe, developed by Pennington et al. at Stanford, takes a different approach—it constructs a global word co-occurrence matrix from the entire corpus, then factorizes this matrix to produce embeddings. The objective function minimizes the difference between the dot product of word vectors and the logarithm of their co-occurrence count. This explicitly captures global corpus statistics rather than just local context windows.

The key differences: Word2Vec is a predictive model (neural network), while GloVe is based on count-based matrix factorization. Word2Vec processes the corpus sequentially through context windows, while GloVe aggregates statistics across the entire corpus first. In practice, the quality of embeddings is similar for both methods when trained on large corpora with sufficient dimensions. Both capture semantic relationships and analogies effectively.

Word2Vec tends to be faster to train on single machines and handles streaming data well. GloVe requires constructing the co-occurrence matrix first, which needs more memory but can be parallelized. GloVe explicitly models co-occurrence ratios, which theoretically better captures certain word relationships. FastText extends Word2Vec with subword information, handling out-of-vocabulary words better. In modern practice, all three are largely superseded by contextual embeddings from BERT and GPT, which generate context-dependent representations rather than fixed vectors per word.
